The ledger

The ledger

Each permission is one line your code keeps: your row, the 32 random bytes that sealed it, and STK's receipt of the seal with its signature. STK has only the seal and its time, and keeps no line.

What a receipt holds

Field
vThe form of the receipt: 2, with both times; 1, from before 2026-11-21 or from a call with the seal alone, without recorded_ms.
sealSHA-256 of the 32 random bytes followed by your row: 64 hex. With recorded_ms, the only thing of yours STK received.
time_msWhen STK signed it, in milliseconds since 1970 (UTC). What STK attests, and the day it is billed for.
recorded_msWhen your library recorded the action, in milliseconds since 1970 (UTC): your own claim, not STK's. The same moment as time_ms unless the seal waited in the library's queue while STK could not be reached.
rule_versionThe SHA-256 of the full text of the rules in use when STK signed it. The rules
accountYour account's id at STK.
which_keytest or live. Only live is billed.
kidThe key that signed it: one of the two keys made for its day (UTC), 200 days ahead; a server that starts again signs with the same keys.

Beside the receipt, in what your code keeps: row (your row, as the text that was sealed: intent, trigger and any fields of yours), nonce (the 32 random bytes, hex), sig, and cancelled when your file told STK a person has it before it answered (newer rules say escalate, or it could not be kept).

How to check a row

Your rows are only on your side. STK holds not one of them. Keep the files the library saved: if you lose one, that permission can no longer be checked afterwards.

stk_verify.py (Python 3 with pip install cryptography) checks each line alone, three things, no secret needed:

  1. The seal. SHA-256 of the random bytes and the row is the seal in the receipt. A row changed after it was sealed does not match.
  2. The signature. The public key of the receipt's kid, from GET https://api.stk-evidence.com/v1/rules?keys=1 or /keys.json, checks STK's Ed25519 signature over the receipt's values in the order above, as a JSON array with no spaces.
  3. The rules. The version named in the receipt, its text signed by STK (GET /v1/rules?history=1), answers finish for the row's intent and trigger. A row with a Never intent, an intent that version closed (closed names), or one that only an older version would pass, does not.

A key signs for one day only; its private half stays in Stripe, encrypted, and STK signs with it only on its day. Its public key stays published, so a permission of years ago is checked with its day's key.

What a receipt proves, and what it does not

Proves:

  • STK signed this seal, at this time, under this version of the rules, with this key.
  • With the row and its random bytes, that the row has not changed since it was signed.
  • The version of the rules is public, so anyone can run the same judgment again and get the same answer.

Does not prove:

  • That every conversation was sealed. A company can choose not to call STK.
  • That the row is a true record of the conversation.
  • That a person actually took over, or what that person did.
  • That the time is attested by anyone other than STK.
  • When the action happened, if the receipt's two times differ: only when STK signed.
  • That STK's handling of its signing keys has been audited. It has not.
  • That the rules are sufficient for any one company's legal duties. Each company remains responsible for its own.

STK attests to the time STK signed. The time the library recorded the action is the customer's own claim. In normal operation these are the same moment. When STK could not be reached, they differ, and the receipt shows both.

A receipt and its signature only

With a receipt and its signature, and no row and no random bytes, anyone can check whether STK signed it:

python3 stk_verify.py --receipt receipt.json keys.json

receipt.json is {"receipt": {...}, "sig": "..."} as STK returned them. keys.json is a copy of STK's public keys (/keys.json); with it, nothing goes over the network. It tells one thing: that STK signed this seal, at this time, under this version of the rules, for this account. It cannot tell what the row was: the intent and the trigger are not in a receipt.

What a company can give a person, for the whole check

A company can give a person affected by an act of its AI these five things, in one line of JSON:

{"row": "<the row, as the text that was sealed>", "nonce": "<the 32 random bytes, 64 hex>",
 "receipt": {...}, "sig": "<base64>", "rule_version": "<the version of the rules in the receipt>"}

With the signed text of that version of the rules and STK's public key of that day in the same file ({"rules": <one item of "versions">} from GET /v1/rules?history=1 and {"key": ...} from /keys.json), python3 stk_verify.py --from-file person.jsonl needs nothing else. A person given these five things can check, asking neither the company nor STK, whether the row the company declared was permitted by the rules (what a receipt proves, and what it does not). How the company gives them is the company's choice; STK asks for nothing.

Seven years later

The same three checks, the same way: python3 stk_verify.py <file.jsonl>. Public keys are never deleted, and every version of the rules stays in GET /v1/rules?history=1, so what you kept on any day checks with its own day's key and its own Rule.

If STK is gone

stk.py and stk.mjs already keep, beside your lines, the signed Rule they applied and the public key of every receipt they got, as they read them from STK at the time. python3 stk_verify.py --from-file <file.jsonl> checks your lines with those and nothing of STK running (put a month's files together into one first). A copy of every public key is also at /keys.json.

Where a forgery fails

A row changed after it was sealed (another intent, another field)Check 1: the seal does not match.
A receipt changed (another time, account or Rule)Check 2: the signature does not match.
A receipt not made by STKCheck 2: no public key of STK's checks it.
A row the rules do not permit, sealed anyway (a Never intent, a trigger other than unknown)Check 3: its Rule does not answer finish.
A row that only an older Rule would permitCheck 3: the receipt names the Rule in use when it was signed.
A day whose signing key STK made invalidCheck 2: that key is marked invalid.

When a check fails

That line is not a permission STK can stand behind: leave it out of your count, and out of any reply to the invoice. One marked cancelled that fails check 3 is expected: your file told STK a person had it, and it was taken off the bill.

Where a receipt comes from

A receipt is STK's answer to POST /v1/ask: receipt and sig, with the rules in use and the grant. stk.py and stk.mjs write it into the line of that action, beside your row and its random bytes, at once; a seal that waited in the queue gets its receipt when STK answers again.

A receipt and an invoice

A receipt is one permission: STK's signature that one action was decided by a named version of the rules, at a time, for your account. It is kept by you, never by STK, and checks without STK. An invoice is a bill: Stripe's, for a week (by card) or a month (by invoice), of $5 for each permission STK signed for your live key, less those a person joined the same day. Each line of the invoice's count is a receipt in your file; a receipt of a test key, or of a permission a person joined in time (the same day, or within 10 minutes after it ended), is on no invoice.

Where your lines are kept

stk.py and stk.mjs write each line to stk-rows/<day>.jsonl next to your code, or give it to a function of yours (for a place where files are not kept, like a serverless function), or, if the folder cannot be written, print it to standard output. They also keep the signed rules they applied, the public key of each receipt, and a joined line each time they tell STK a person has a conversation. Each permission's line is kept before ask() returns. A permission that arrives after your code stopped waiting is kept as it came, followed by a joined line with its seal; if STK cannot be told then, a to_tell line first, and the joined line when STK answers. An intent on no list of the rules leaves a not_listed line, for your own count; STK is not told.
